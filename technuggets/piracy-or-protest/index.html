Piracy or Protest?

What are the tradeoffs of the mantra: "if payment is not ownership, piracy is not theft"

BOBolade OdeniyiLead Contributor, HOUSE603 · Systems Architecture & Security
Published 20 Nov 2026 · 10 min read

Quick answer

Legally, the popular internet slogan captures one technical truth and hides two expensive traps. Copyright infringement is indeed legally distinct from physical theft, as courts treat unauthorised copying differently from depriving an owner of physical goods. However, "not theft" does not mean lawful, and more urgently for everyday users, it does not mean safe. With the console-exclusive launch of Grand Theft Auto VI on 19 November 2026 and zero official PC version available, threat actors are exploiting frustrated players with trojanised "PC crack" torrents carrying Warden Stealer, Remote Access Trojans, and destructive ransomware.

Every few years, a single internet slogan captures years of accumulated consumer frustration in a dozen words. In modern software, gaming, and streaming debates, few phrases travel faster than this one: "If payment is not ownership, piracy is not theft."

At first glance, the logic feels symmetrical and biting. If major digital storefronts take your money at checkout while burying clauses in End User Licence Agreements stating that you only hold a revocable licence to access the software, critics argue that publishers have abandoned the traditional bargain of ownership. Therefore, the argument goes, obtaining an unlicensed copy is less a crime and more a form of digital civil disobedience.

Beneath the rhetoric, however, sit two colliding realities. First, the slogan rests on a genuine legal distinction between physical theft and copyright infringement, while quietly pretending both concepts carry equal impunity. Second, in the modern threat landscape, acting on that mantra exposes ordinary users and hybrid workplaces to severe infostealer, ransomware, and identity-compromise risks that cost far more than a software licence.

Why the Frustration Behind the Slogan Is Real

Dismissing consumer resentment out of hand misses why the mantra resonated widely. Digital buyers did not invent the grievance out of thin air; corporate licensing practices earned it.

In January 2024, Ubisoft Director of Subscriptions Philippe Tremblay remarked in an interview that consumers would need to grow "comfortable with not owning your games" as distribution models moved further toward subscriptions and cloud services. Just two months later, on 31 March 2024, Ubisoft shut down the servers for its always-online 2014 racing title The Crew, rendering purchased digital and physical discs permanently unplayable and removing the title from user libraries.

The backlash was swift enough to trigger consumer litigation, the international Stop Killing Games preservation campaign, and legislative action. On 1 January 2025, California Assembly Bill 2426 (AB 2426) took effect under California Business and Professions Code Section 17500.6, barring digital storefronts from advertising transactions with words such as "buy" or "purchase" unless the buyer receives permanent offline ownership or sees a clear pre-checkout disclosure explaining that access is merely a revocable licence.

Consumers were right to demand honest labelling. Paying retail prices for media that can vanish when a backend server retires or a licensing agreement lapses is a legitimate consumer-protection issue. Yet resolving that grievance through unlicensed crack sites trades a flawed commercial contract for an unverified binary execution environment.

The Legal Confusion: Why "Not Theft" Is Not the Same as "Permitted"

The philosophical core of "if payment is not ownership, piracy is not theft" treats two distinct legal concepts, theft and copyright infringement, as interchangeable benchmarks for permissibility. Ironically, both sides of the debate routinely misuse the word "theft."

In criminal and property law, physical theft (larceny or conversion) requires taking tangible property in a manner that deprives the rightful owner of possession. If someone takes a boxed console disc off a retail shelf, the shop no longer has that disc to sell. By contrast, copyright protects an intangible bundle of statutory rights granted over reproduction, distribution, and public performance.

In the landmark 1985 decision Dowling v. United States, 473 U.S. 207, the United States Supreme Court directly addressed whether unauthorised duplication and distribution of copyrighted works counts as "stolen, converted, or taken by fraud" under the National Stolen Property Act (18 U.S.C. section 2314). Writing for a 6 to 3 majority, Justice Harry Blackmun held that it does not: an infringer creates new copies rather than physically removing or wholly depriving the copyright holder of possession of the underlying work.

Here is where the protest mantra commits its own logical leap. Demonstrating that copyright infringement is not common-law theft does not establish that infringement is lawful, victimless, or immune from penalty. Precisely because intellectual property differs from tangible property, legislatures wrote dedicated statutory frameworks governing infringement:

  • United States: Civil liability under the Copyright Act alongside criminal penalties for willful infringement under 17 U.S.C. section 506 and 18 U.S.C. section 2319.
  • United Kingdom: Civil remedies and specific criminal offences for making or distributing infringing articles under Section 107 of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988.
  • Nigeria: Modernised statutory prohibitions covering unauthorised commercial reproduction, online distribution, and anti-circumvention of technological protection measures under the Copyright Act, 2022 enforced alongside the Nigerian Copyright Commission.

Case Study: The November 2026 GTA 6 Launch and the Fake PC Crack Wave

Legal theory matters in courtrooms, but the immediate price of the "piracy as protest" mindset is paid on everyday laptops and home networks. The global launch of Grand Theft Auto VI (GTA 6) illustrates how cybercriminals systematically exploit moral rationalisation and release-window hype.

On 19 November 2026, Rockstar Games officially released GTA 6 worldwide following digital pre-loads that opened on 10 November 2026. The launch arrived exclusively for PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S, returning players to Leonida and Vice City. Crucially, no PC edition was released at launch, nor has Rockstar Games announced an official PC release date.

That single engineering fact makes every website, torrent index, Discord invite, or forum post claiming to host a "GTA 6 PC crack," "early access PC build," or "leaked PC dev torrent" mathematically simple to classify: 100 percent are fraudulent lures.

Despite that impossibility, security researchers across Cynet, Huntress, ESET, Malwarebytes, and NordVPN Threat Intelligence documented surging attack campaigns right through launch week targeting players unwilling to wait for an official PC port or convinced that bypassing retail channels is harmless. Threat actors deployed dark-web scam kits alongside search-engine-optimised landing pages, rogue Google Colab notebooks, and artificially padded 100 GB to 209 GB decoy torrents engineered to look convincing by file size alone.

Inside those counterfeit installers sit four distinct classes of high-impact malware:

  1. Browser & Credential Infostealers (Warden Stealer, Vidar): Cynet analysts documented fake GTA 6 links dropping Warden Stealer, a compact infostealer built to harvest saved browser passwords, active session cookies, Steam and gaming accounts, and browser-extension crypto wallet data within seconds of execution.
  2. Remote Access Trojans (njRAT, DCRat): Huntress and endpoint defenders observed bloated torrent packages installing persistent Remote Access Trojans alongside destructive file wipers and ransomware capable of locking personal documents across local drives.
  3. Clipboard Hijackers: Once resident in memory, hidden background scripts monitor Windows clipboard activity, silently replacing copied cryptocurrency wallet strings with an attacker's wallet address moments before transfer confirmation.
  4. Antivirus Disarmament via Social Engineering: Almost every fake crack bundle includes a plausible-sounding README.txt warning users that security scanners emit "false positives against DRM bypass tools," instructing the victim to disable Windows Defender, whitelist the Downloads folder, and run setup.exe as an administrator.

Why Session Theft Turns a Home Gaming Mistake into a Corporate Breach

In remote and hybrid engineering organisations, personal Windows desktops routinely serve double duty. The same browser profile used at midnight to browse gaming forums often holds active corporate Slack, Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, GitHub, or cloud console session tokens by morning.

That convergence explains why infostealers are dangerous even when multi-factor authentication is enabled. Tools like Warden Stealer do not need to crack your 20-character password or intercept an SMS code; they simply exfiltrate the decrypted browser session cookies stored inside your active Windows user profile. Once an attacker replays a live session token from their own machine, MFA challenges are bypassed entirely until explicit server-side session revocation occurs.

What Network Security Tools Can (and Cannot) Stop

It is worth stating plainly where network privacy tools stop. Running a VPN does not disarm an executable once you download a ZIP archive, disable Windows Defender, and double-click an infected installer yourself. Network encryption protects packets in transit; it cannot inspect inside your local execution sandbox when you grant administrator rights to unknown code.

Where network and browser-level defensive layers do help is upstream in the attack funnel, before a malicious binary touches local storage. SEO-poisoned download portals, malvertising redirects, and phishing clones rely on freshly registered or flagged domains to lure impatient searchers. Tools combining encrypted WireGuard tunnels with built-in malicious URL and tracker blocking, such as NordVPN using Threat Protection Pro, can flag and interrupt connections to known phishing hosts, command-and-control endpoints, and suspicious file downloads before execution while shielding ISP-visible browsing activity on untrusted connections. Treated as an upstream barrier alongside intact operating-system antivirus and disciplined browser isolation, URL filtering catches mistakes early rather than cleaning up after a wipe.

Constructive Alternatives to Trading Security for "Protest"

Criticising broken digital licensing models is entirely justified, and pushing publishers toward transparent ownership rights does not require handing your passwords to cybercriminals. Readers and teams looking for durable tradeoffs have far better options:

  • Buy from DRM-free platforms where offline ownership is genuine: Storefronts such as GOG provide standalone, offline-capable installers that continue working even if storefront servers vanish decades later, which directly satisfies California AB 2426's permanent-download exemption.
  • Support legislative disclosure and preservation initiatives: Laws such as AB 2426 and European consumer digital-content directives force storefronts to label temporary licences honestly rather than masking them as permanent purchases, shifting incentives without compromising endpoint security.
  • Keep work identities off personal entertainment hardware: Under zero-trust and ISO/IEC 27001 hygiene, employees should never sign into corporate SaaS, password managers, or production cloud consoles on hardware where unofficial binaries or mods are executed.
  • Treat any "disable antivirus" instruction as confirmation of malware: Legitimate software distributors sign binaries with verifiable publisher certificates; instructions asking you to turn off Defender to play an unreleased PC port are always hostile.

The debate over digital ownership deserves real legal reform, consumer pressure, and preservation engineering. Confusing copyright infringement with physical theft does not help publishers build trust, and confusing ideological protest with cybersecurity safety only enriches the malware operators waiting on the other side of a fake download link.
